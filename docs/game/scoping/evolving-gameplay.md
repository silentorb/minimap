## Brutal sandbox

While there are some games that could be described as "brutal sandboxes" (such as Noita), most sandbox games I've played are very forgiving.

I'd like to make a sandbox game that is very challenging, but similar to the spirit of sandboxes, the challenge is also very open-ended.

That is further balanced by "Dynamic difficulty selection" further below.

## Loose win conditions

Instead of having a single win condition, having multiple win conditions that are:
1. Not mutually exclusive
2. Do not immediately end the game when met

The primary motivations for this is to make win/loss a complex gradient instead of a single boolean win/loss condition.

When a game has a single boolean win/loss condition, the challenge is also effectively a boolean challenge: a player either passes the challenge or fails the challenge.

I want a game with a variety of degrees and directions of success.

Players can choose which goals they wish to pursue.

This is similar to the diegetic game mode configuration.  Instead of players choosing up front what the win conditions are, they can personally decide.

This is a variation of how I've seen some players play games, where they talk about inventing their own goals.

This is similar, except these are predefined goals that are formally awknowledged by the game.

They are similar to achievements, but handled and framed differently.

## Diegetic difficulty selection

In a way this is just a combination of the previous points, except this ties them together and adds its own flourish.

This idea is primarily inspired by the Mordheim PC game.

In that game, the game can be as easy or difficult as the player wants things to be.  If the player wants, the player can always play it safe.

However, playing it safe is slower, and the game strongly incentivizes the player to take chances.  The game both dangles carrots in front of the player to take risks for, praises the player when he successfully achieves that carrot, and haunts the player with the missed opportunity when the player doesn't get the carrot.

It's risk vs. reward, except it's unboxed.  Normally risk vs. reward is described in the context of a larger singular win/loss condition.  So risk vs. reward usually boils down to "increase risk now to reduce risk later."  Often with such games, if the player does not take enough early risks, the later game may be impossible.

Mordheim isn't like that.  It's risks are entirely optional.  But you're not really playing the game if you don't take a minimal amount of them.

It fits the sandbox spirit to have a game where players are tempted to ramp up the difficulty.

But the difficulty is also dynamic.  Players can choose during gameplay whether to ramp up the difficulty or reduce it.  And they don't choose by using an external menu UI.  They control the difficulty through the in-game actions they perform.

And there is still risk vs. reward, but here the reward is different win conditions (achievements).
